How the SNAP screening works

This is a short screening (less than 2 minutes) that helps someone on SNAP figure out whether the ABAWD work rules might apply to them. It asks a few plain-language questions and, based only on the answers, shows one of three outcomes. Nothing is sent anywhere. Answers stay in the browser tab and are erased when it closes. See Privacy below.

What are the "ABAWD work rules"?

ABAWD stands for "Able-Bodied Adult Without Dependents." Federal rules can require some SNAP recipients in this group to work, train, or volunteer a set number of hours each month to keep their benefits. But many people are exempt, and others have a good reason for months they couldn't meet the hours. The screening exists because a lot of people are told they must meet the rules when they actually don't have to.

The start page explains that these rules generally apply to adults 18 through 64. There is no age question and no separate age result; everyone goes through the same questions.

The possible outcomes

Exempt: you may not have to meet the work rules
The person picked at least one exemption reason (for example, living with a child under 14, a health reason, or earning enough income). The result lists those reasons, tells them to contact DTA as soon as they can, and explains what proof to send for work-, housing-, or disability-based exemptions. The word exempt in the heading can be hovered or tapped for a short reminder of what it means. A fill-in statement form can be printed, downloaded, or emailed to themselves, then signed and sent to DTA.
Good cause: you may have a good reason for missing hours
No exemption, but a temporary hardship may excuse months of missed hours. The result lists transportation, emergency, and employment-issue examples (including before or after a start date) and offers a statement form. The person is told to reach DTA as soon as they can.
May need to meet the work rules
No exemption and no good cause came up. The result explains that the person may need to meet the rules through paid work, unpaid work, or a training program for 20 hours a week (80 hours a month), or through community service for a set number of hours each month. It also explains how to tell DTA they are meeting the rules, including proof for paid work and a signed statement for unpaid work, plus how to raise good cause, start the screening over, or reapply if SNAP was lost.

Why three result screens?

The proposal centers on three main results: exempt, good cause, or must meet the rules. The draft now keeps 30+ hours/week at lower pay inside the exempt result, because current guidance treats that work situation as an employment/income exemption category.

The work question asks whether someone is working enough. These answers point to an exemption, but the person should still tell DTA and send proof of income and hours:

  • Exempt (income): earning $217.50+/week or 14.5 hours at $15+/hour.
  • Exempt (hours): working 30+ hours/week at lower pay.

How the decision order works

The screening runs entirely in the browser. It checks answers in this order and stops at the first result that fits:

1
Any exemption?

If any exemption answer is selected → Exempt. See the list below.

↓ if no exemption
2
Good cause hardship?

A final question asks whether something is making it hard to work, go to school, or volunteer right now (transportation, an emergency, or an unfair job). If yes → Good cause.

↓ if no good cause
3
Nothing above applies

→ May need to meet the work rules.

How the letter to DTA gets written

Both the Exempt and Good cause screens end with a letter the person can print, download, or email to themselves, then sign and send to DTA. The tool never sends it; it only writes it.

Most of that letter is fixed: the date, the DTA address block, the opening paragraph, and the bulleted list of reasons. What the person adds is the middle: a few sentences in their own words for each reason that needs explaining, plus their name, client or agency ID if they have one, and a signature.

  1. The screening finishes

    Exempt or good cause. Anyone who may need to meet the work rules gets no letter, so nothing below happens to them.

    2. ↓
  2. Which reasons need explaining?

    Some exemptions speak for themselves (pregnant, TAFDC, school, and others). The rest get one empty box each, with a prompt above it, such as explaining a health reason, caretaking, work hours and pay, housing, or why hours were missed.

    3. ↓
  3. They fill in the boxes, name, and signature

    The same privacy callout from the start page appears again above the form: Your information is private. MLRI will not save any personal information you share on this form. A ← Back button returns to the last question screen with answers intact if they want to change something.

    4. ↓
  4. Print, download, or email

    Print or save this form opens the letter alone, not the web page. Download as Word saves a file with the signature as a picture. Email myself a copy opens a panel where they can enter an address; sending from the page is not set up yet, so Open in my email app instead is the working path today.

    5. ↓
  5. What the printed letter looks like

    Each reason stays in the bulleted list. What they typed appears in an indented box under the matching bullet, not as a repeated prompt line. Fixed paragraphs (for work, disability, or housing) sit the same way when those apply.

Hardest ask. The write-in boxes land last, after someone already believes they are finished. A health reason, caretaking, or housing situation can mean explaining something personal in a few sentences. That is deliberate: DTA expects the person's own account, not a checkbox summary.

What counts as an exemption?

Answering yes to any of these (in the question flow) can lead to an Exempt result. They are grouped like this to keep the explainer readable:

Your family and household

  • Live with a child under 14
  • Take care of a child under 6
  • Take care of someone who cannot care for themselves
  • Pregnant

Health and safety

  • Health reason that makes 30+ hrs/week hard
  • Domestic violence, stalking, sexual harassment, sexual assault, or another safety situation
  • No regular place to sleep, plus follow-up answers DTA should review for unable-to-work factors

Public benefits and participating in programs

  • Getting or applying for TAFDC
  • Getting or applying for unemployment
  • Disability benefits listed, or another disability benefit/payment DTA should review
  • Substance use treatment program
  • Services from state agencies (listed under the question)

School, work, and background

  • Enrolled in school half-time or more
  • Tribal membership (as described in the tool)
  • Earn $217.50+/week, 14.5 hrs/week at $15+/hour, or 30+ hrs/week below minimum wage

Note on housing: Saying "no regular place to sleep" is not treated as automatic. A follow-up asks about diploma, health care, work or school history, and recent hospitalization, and the result tells the person DTA should review those unable-to-work factors.

Note on disability: Selecting "Other disability benefit or payment" routes to a cautious tell-DTA path, and the statement asks the person to name the benefit or payment.

Questions to address

These are the open legal/content points for MLRI or DTA practice experts to address before launch:

  • Should working 30+ hours/week while earning less than minimum wage always route to the exempt result, and what proof should the statement ask for?
  • For "Other disability benefit or payment," should the tool route to exempt as a cautious tell-DTA path, or should it ask a follow-up before showing an exemption result?
  • For no regular place to sleep, is the current "DTA should review unable-to-work factors" wording accurate enough, or should the result say "may be exempt" more prominently?
  • Is the substance use treatment question broad enough, or should it name specific treatment/program participation criteria?

Privacy

There is no backend. Nothing anyone types is sent anywhere, and MLRI never receives it. There are currently no analytics of any kind, though that is expected to change to aggregate counts that record no answers and no identifiers.

The version going to MassLegalHelp keeps answers for the browser tab only. They survive a refresh, and they are erased when the tab closes, with nothing recoverable afterwards. This is deliberate: the questions cover pregnancy, disability, substance use treatment, and domestic violence, and the assumption is that the phone may be shared or monitored. It costs the ability to come back tomorrow and finish, which is the right way round for a screening that takes less than 2 minutes.

On the start page and on the statement form, the screener shows the same privacy callout: Your information is private. MLRI will not save any personal information you share on this form.

There is also a Quick exit control in the top bar. It erases the stored answers and then leaves the page, replacing the current history entry so the Back button cannot return to them.

What someone types on the "Tell DTA" statement, their name, any client or agency ID, the explanation, and the signature, is never stored at all. It is read from the page only at the moment they print, download, or email. To keep a copy, that is what they do.
