How the SNAP screening works

This is a short screening that helps someone on SNAP figure out whether the ABAWD work rules might apply to them. It asks a few plain-language questions and, based only on the answers, shows one of several outcomes. Nothing is sent anywhere. Answers stay in the browser tab and are erased when it closes. See Privacy below.

What are the "ABAWD work rules"?

ABAWD stands for "Able-Bodied Adult Without Dependents." Federal rules can require some SNAP recipients in this group to work, train, or volunteer a set number of hours each month to keep their benefits. But many people are exempt, and others have a good reason for months they couldn't meet the hours. The screening exists because a lot of people are told they must meet the rules when they actually don't have to.

The possible outcomes

Exempt: you may not have to meet the work rules
The person picked at least one exemption reason (for example, living with a child under 14, a health reason, or earning enough income). The result lists those reasons, tells them to contact DTA as soon as they can, and explains what proof to send for work-, housing-, or disability-based exemptions. A fill-in statement form can be printed or downloaded to mail, fax, or upload to DTA Connect.
Good cause: you may have a good reason for missing hours
No exemption, but a temporary hardship may excuse months of missed hours. The result lists transportation, emergency, and employment-issue examples (including before or after a start date) and offers a statement form. The person is told to reach DTA as soon as they can.
May need to meet the work rules
No exemption and no good cause came up. The result explains that the person may need to meet the rules through paid work, unpaid work, or a training program for 20 hours a week (80 hours a month), or through community service for a set number of hours each month. It also explains how to tell DTA they are meeting the rules, including proof for paid work and a signed statement for unpaid work, plus how to raise good cause, start the screening over, or reapply if SNAP was lost.
Age may not apply
If the person is not between 18 and 64, the screening explains that these rules may not apply and suggests contacting DTA or legal aid.

Why three main result screens?

The proposal centers on three main results: exempt, good cause, or must meet the rules. The draft now keeps 30+ hours/week at lower pay inside the exempt result, because current guidance treats that work situation as an employment/income exemption category.

The work question asks whether someone is working enough. These answers point to an exemption, but the person should still tell DTA and send proof of income and hours:

  • Exempt (income): earning $217.50+/week or 14.5 hours at $15+/hour.
  • Exempt (hours): working 30+ hours/week at lower pay.

Age may not apply is an early result. It appears if the person is not 18–64 and stops the screening before the questions.

How the decision order works

The screening runs entirely in the browser. It checks answers in this order and stops at the first result that fits:

1
Age check (intro)

If the person is not 18–64 → Age may not apply. The question flow stops here.

↓ if age 18–64
2
Any exemption?

If any exemption answer is selected → Exempt. See the list below.

↓ if no exemption
3
Good cause hardship?

A final question asks whether something is making it hard to work, go to school, or volunteer right now (transportation, an emergency, or an unfair job). If yes → Good cause.

↓ if no good cause
4
Nothing above applies

→ May need to meet the work rules.

How the letter to DTA gets written

Both the Exempt and Good cause screens end with a letter the person can print, download, or email, then sign and send to DTA. The tool never sends it; it only writes it.

Most of that letter is fixed. The part that is not is the middle: the person's own account of their situation, in their own voice. There are two versions of that middle part, and choosing between them is an open question for the team and for DTA.

 Version A, shipping todayVersion B, under review
What they seeOne empty box per exemption, with a prompt above itTwo or three multiple-choice questions, then the finished sentences
What they typeA few sentences, in their own wordsNothing
Extra questionsNone2 to 5, on one screen before the results

Version A asks someone to explain their own health, caretaking, or housing under a prompt like "Explain the health reason that makes it hard for you to work 30 or more hours a week." It is the hardest thing the tool asks of anyone and it lands last, after they already believe they are finished. Version B asks a few more multiple-choice questions instead and writes the sentences from the answers.

Version B works like this.

  1. The screening finishes

    Exempt or good cause. Anyone who may need to meet the work rules gets no letter, so nothing below happens to them.

    2. ↓
  2. Which of their reasons need explaining?

    Each reason is looked at on its own. Some carry all the information DTA needs already; some do not.

    3. ↓
    If it speaks for itself
    Ask nothing

    Pregnant, TAFDC, a Tribe, school, unemployment, a safety situation, substance use treatment, or a named disability benefit. There is nothing to add, so nothing is asked and the reason simply appears in the letter's list.

    If it needs explaining
    Ask two or three multiple-choice questions

    A health reason, caretaking, a child under 6, working, another disability benefit, or having no regular place to sleep. These are the six DTA needs detail on, and they are the same six Version A puts a blank box under.

    ↓   both paths rejoin   ↓
  3. Write one paragraph per reason that needed explaining

    Built from the answers, one sentence at a time. A skipped question drops its sentence rather than being guessed at, so a half-answered case still produces a true paragraph, just a shorter one.

    4. ↓
  4. Take that reason out of the bulleted list

    The paragraph already says it, and says more. Without this the letter states the same reason twice: once as a bullet and again as the opening words of the paragraph beneath it.

    5. ↓
  5. Show the person what was written

    In full, on screen, above the signature, with a link back to change any answer. Nobody signs a letter they have not read.

    6. ↓
  6. They add their name and sign

    The two things the tool cannot write. Then print, download as Word, or email it to themselves.

A sentence being built

Someone who said they have a health reason that makes it hard to work 30 hours a week is asked three questions. Each answer adds to the same paragraph:

  • A physical health reason I have a physical health condition that makes it hard for me to work 30 or more hours a week.
  • 6 months or more …as above, then It has lasted 6 months or more, or I expect it to.
  • Yes, I see a provider regularly …then I see a health care provider for it regularly, and I can ask them for a letter if you need one.

Three taps instead of a blank box. The same person in Version A sees an empty field and the instruction to explain it themselves.

Two things still open. The first is whether DTA will accept a statement composed by the tool rather than written by the claimant. The second matters more and is a question for lawyers rather than designers: a composed statement still sits above someone's signature, so is it still their statement? Showing them the finished text with a way to change it is the safeguard, not an answer. Until both are settled, Version A is what the public would get.
Where multiple choice is worse. When someone gets a disability benefit that was not on the list, Version A asks them to name it and takes any answer. No list can hold every disability payment there is, so Version B offers the common ones and then falls back to "I receive a disability benefit or payment that was not on the list. I will bring the paperwork so you can review it." That is weaker than a written answer. It is the clearest single case for keeping one box.

What counts as an exemption?

Answering yes to any of these (in the question flow) can lead to an Exempt result. They are grouped like this to keep the explainer readable:

Your family and household

  • Live with a child under 14
  • Take care of a child under 6
  • Take care of someone who cannot care for themselves
  • Pregnant

Health and safety

  • Health reason that makes 30+ hrs/week hard
  • Domestic violence, stalking, sexual harassment, sexual assault, or another safety situation
  • No regular place to sleep, plus follow-up answers DTA should review for unable-to-work factors

Public benefits and participating in programs

  • Getting or applying for TAFDC
  • Getting or applying for unemployment
  • Disability benefits listed, or another disability benefit/payment DTA should review
  • Substance use treatment program
  • Services from certain state agencies

School, work, and background

  • Enrolled in school half-time or more
  • Tribal membership (as described in the tool)
  • Earn $217.50+/week, 14.5 hrs/week at $15+/hour, or 30+ hrs/week below minimum wage

Note on housing: Saying "no regular place to sleep" is not treated as automatic. A follow-up asks about diploma, health care, work or school history, and recent hospitalization, and the result tells the person DTA should review those unable-to-work factors.

Note on disability: Selecting "Other disability benefit or payment" routes to a cautious tell-DTA path, and the statement asks the person to name the benefit or payment.

Questions to address

These are the open legal/content points for MLRI or DTA practice experts to address before launch:

  • Should working 30+ hours/week while earning less than minimum wage always route to the exempt result, and what proof should the statement ask for?
  • For "Other disability benefit or payment," should the tool route to exempt as a cautious tell-DTA path, or should it ask a follow-up before showing an exemption result?
  • For no regular place to sleep, is the current "DTA should review unable-to-work factors" wording accurate enough, or should the result say "may be exempt" more prominently?
  • Is the substance use treatment question broad enough, or should it name specific treatment/program participation criteria?

Privacy

There is no backend. Nothing anyone types is sent anywhere, and MLRI never receives it. There are currently no analytics of any kind, though that is expected to change to aggregate counts that record no answers and no identifiers.

The version going to MassLegalHelp keeps answers for the browser tab only. They survive a refresh, and they are erased when the tab closes, with nothing recoverable afterwards. This is deliberate: the questions cover pregnancy, disability, substance use treatment, and domestic violence, and the assumption is that the phone may be shared or monitored. It costs the ability to come back tomorrow and finish, which is the right way round for a screening that takes three minutes.

There is also a Quick exit control in the top bar. It erases the stored answers and then leaves the page, replacing the current history entry so the Back button cannot return to them.

What someone types on the "Tell DTA" statement, their name, any client or agency ID, the explanation, and the signature, is never stored at all. It is read from the page only at the moment they print, download, or email. To keep a copy, that is what they do.

This is a draft explainer, not legal advice. Results depend only on the answers given, and may not be complete or correct. People should always check with DTA before acting, and can reach a legal aid program (like MLRI) for advice. Anything here that doesn't match how DTA handles a case should be flagged for review.
